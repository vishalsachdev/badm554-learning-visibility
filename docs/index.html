Making Student Reasoning Visible

How GitHub, AI-assisted review and Canvas can show how students think, not only what they hand in. What one course's records show, and what they do not show yet.

Vishal Sachdev · Gies College of Business, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign · October 2026

The short version

  • The main result is a working course method. Student work lives in GitHub, where every version is kept. AI and scripts make it practical to inspect that work again and again. Canvas stays the place for messages and grades.
  • The records show the method working. They show checks of student work, a wrong flag that was caught and corrected, targeted messages to students, and updates that were confirmed in Canvas.
  • They do not show that students learned more, or that faculty saved time. Those are the next things to measure. The most useful next piece of evidence is one complete feedback loop: a student gets feedback, revises the work and explains the change.

Behind the visible course

The faculty workflow

Five repeatable steps: specify the evidence, collect it, interpret it, act on what was approved, then read back and close the loop. The instructor approves anything with consequences. Show the five steps

The hidden work is a repeated sequence: collect evidence, interpret it, decide, and confirm the follow-through. The sequence can be reused week to week, while the teaching criteria stay specific to each course. The steps below come from the course records, except the second half of step 5, which the records do not yet show consistently.

  1. Specify the evidence

    Turn the assignment into things that can be inspected: a prediction before AI, a required pipeline, a check with a stated blind spot, a checkpoint. A rubric and a starter file make the request clear to students and to the reviewing tools.

  2. Collect and reconcile

    Scripts gather the work and its history. Matching against the roster separates active students from staff commits, alternate usernames and students who dropped. A failed read counts as unknown evidence, never as missing work.

  3. Inspect and interpret

    Simple checks confirm files, timing and required sections. An AI-assisted review reads explanations and code in context. The instructor checks any interpretation with consequences, and may decide the assignment wording caused the gap, not the student.

  4. Approve and act

    Draft targeted messages or grade updates, review the exceptions, and apply only what was approved. The Module 6 and Module 7 cases show instructor approval at this step, not an unattended process.

  5. Read back and close the loop

    Confirm that the message, grade entry or content change exists. Then, in a later check, look at the student's response and revised work. The first confirmation shows the task was done. The second is what shows learning.

What each component does

Skills write down the reusable procedure and its safeguards. Scripts make collection, comparison and delivery repeatable. AI helps read varied student work and draft specific feedback. Canvas MCP, an open-source tool the author built, lets AI agents read from and write to Canvas, so this work connects to assignments, submissions, discussions and messages.8 It is a connection layer, not a new Canvas analytics feature.

Two cautions. Not every step used Canvas MCP: the discussion replies were posted with a separate script. And having a tool does not mean the work runs unattended. The discussion record shows an approved batch, a failure partway through, renewed permission and a final check. The course's audit notes set out a schedule and a reusable skill, but a planned schedule is not proof of a reliable recurring service.

Where the criteria come from

The design principles of the Gies online Master of Science in Business Analytics treat AI as a thinking partner and productivity aid, and ask students to form their own hypotheses, critique and reflect before and after using it. The program's assessment strategy recommends a few natural and reflective records of the work process, such as revisions, debugging notes and AI attribution logs. It also recommends a rubric dimension for the learning trajectory that rewards reasoned changes of direction and recovery from errors. These are recommendations. They should not be read as already in place in every course.1

The program's GitHub strategy goes further. It proposes version-controlled professional practice as a program learning outcome: working incrementally, collaborating, evaluating what AI contributed and presenting evidence. Program leadership has not yet decided on that proposal. BADM 554 can illustrate it, but does not show program-wide adoption.

Interpretation

Git as a mental model for knowledge work

Commits, branches, issues, reviews and tests can teach students to treat analysis as work that can be revised and inspected. The value is in the explanation attached to each change. Show the five practices

Interpretation This is an educational reading of the course design, supported by the proposed program outcome and by the prediction-and-revision designs in BADM 554. It does not claim that a commit history shows what a student was thinking.

Redrawn from a mind map that Google NotebookLM generated from this report. A sixth practice, readable history, sits under all five: leave enough context for another person or AI agent to continue the work without reconstructing every decision.

The benefit comes from the explanation attached to the change. A useful window into learning joins a first claim, a test or critique, a changed piece of work and the student's own explanation. A diff alone shows that text changed. It does not show why, who understood it, or whether AI wrote it. The program's GitHub strategy itself names always-on AI code completion as a problem for any claim of unaided work.

Students should be able to understand and challenge the record. Tell them which evidence is collected and why. Let them correct identity matches and point to valid work done elsewhere. Use a few meaningful checkpoints instead of rewarding commit counts. The course's own audit notes warn that commits do not measure effort or quality, that alternate usernames and paired work make attribution hard, and that work done in Word or SharePoint may never reach GitHub. Students should learn to document their work honestly, not to generate activity for a dashboard.

Evidence can stay private and still be reviewed by authorized faculty and tools. In BADM 554, team repositories are private for the term. Making student work private by default across the whole program, with publication to a portfolio as the student's own choice, is still a proposal.

A perspective for the learning team

A proposal: the same evidence workflow could help course design staff check that instructions, materials and follow-up work as intended. Course quality first, not student monitoring. Show the proposal

This is a proposal based on the same course records. It does not claim these staff workflows exist, that roles have changed, or that a pilot has been approved.

The same evidence workflow could help the learning team, the staff who support course design and delivery, check how courses are built and run. The focus would be the course, not individual students.

Staff questionEvidence and possible action
Are the instructions producing the intended work?Compare the brief, the starter file and the structure of what students submit. In Module 6, an unclear submission expectation and a wrong flag showed up. That argues for a design review before treating students as non-compliant.
Did the approved materials reach the right people?Check roster matching, file versions and publication records. The Module 7 distribution caught identity mismatches before delivery and verified 24 student copies.
Are interventions actually complete?Track approval, send result and confirmation separately. The discussion reply script stopped partway through, so a closed task should record the recovery too.
Is a tool failure distorting the activity data?Separate access, sign-in, runtime and quota problems from student difficulty. The tutor failure report is a service problem until a reliable retest says otherwise.

With the right permissions, Canvas MCP could let staff and AI agents work from course evidence without rebuilding it by hand across screens. Its documented tools cover course structure, assignments and discussions, plus content quality and accessibility checks.8 Running a scanner or a course quality check is not accessibility certification, and it does not prove a course is ready to publish. The program's assessment principles should still decide what is collected. Staff can help make evidence expectations clear and consistent, while faculty keep the decisions about the learning task, its interpretation and its assessment.

Start with course quality, not student monitoring. A small pilot could look at one weekly package: the assignment, rubric, notebook, Canvas placement and follow-up record. Its output would be a short list of exceptions, each linked to the exact version inspected.

Before release

Check that the assignment and rubric agree, required files exist, links work, students' original prediction work will be kept, and the roster matching sends nothing to the wrong place. Do a dry run before distributing.

During delivery

Separate evidence that could not be reached from work that is incomplete. Send unclear instructions to the faculty owner and technical failures to the delivery owner. Prepare suggestions or drafts without changing assessment rules or contacting students.

After delivery

Confirm publication or delivery in the system of record. Sample feedback and revisions to find recurring design problems. Record what should change next time and the evidence for it.

Measure before expanding

  • Time per weekly review, including preparation, corrections and failures, compared with the current process.
  • False flags and missed defects, with the reason: identity mismatch, unreachable source or unclear instruction.
  • Time from a confirmed course defect to its fix, and whether affected students got a clear remedy.
  • Whether the exception list helps faculty act without adding documentation work for students.

Access and ownership. Use the least course access the work needs. Keep identifiable records inside the authorized staff group, and share only aggregate or student-safe summaries. Name who reviews exceptions and who approves changes with consequences. The course's audit notes already warn about staff accounts, alternate usernames and work done off the platform; those safeguards should travel with any reusable skill.
